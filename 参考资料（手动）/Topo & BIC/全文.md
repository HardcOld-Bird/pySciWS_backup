# Observation of Extreme Anisotropic Sensitivity at Topological Bound States in the Continuum

**Ruizhi Dong,\* Yihuan Zhu,\* Dongxing Mao, Xu Wang,† and Yong Li‡**

*Institute of Acoustics, Tongji University, Shanghai 200092, China*

(Received 18 January 2025; revised 21 March 2025; accepted 1 May 2025; published 19 May 2025)

**Abstract:**

Bound states in the continuum (BICs) are exceptional resonances with extremely high sensitivity and thus inherently fragile. Introducing topological concepts can fortify BICs against perturbations; however, existing topological BICs (TBICs) often rely on intricate strategies. Here, we explore the universality of TBICs governed by symmetry. Different symmetries naturally segregate wave states into uncoupled Hilbert subspaces, enabling independent topological band structure engineering in each subspace. This results in rich topological features, including TBICs, topological Fano resonance, and even exotic TBICs (eTBICs). Intriguingly, eTBICs inherit core attributes from both topological states and BICs, thereby exhibiting counterintuitive behavior—both strong immunity and high sensitivity. We conceptually and experimentally demonstrate this using an acoustic monolayer topological waveguide, uncovering exotic behavior in the transmission spectrum where the bulk is "muted" and the eTBIC is identified with extreme anisotropic sensitivity. Our findings may enrich the research implications of both topological physics and BICs.

## Introduction

In wave physics, bound states in the continuum (BICs) are counterintuitive localized states that have an extremely long lifetime, even though they are embedded within the continuum spectrum of extended states capable of carrying energy away. Initially proposed in quantum mechanics [1], BICs have since been discovered in various wave systems, including electromagnetic [2–13], acoustic [14–18], and water waves [19]. Their origins can be attributed to different mechanisms, such as symmetry incompatibility [6], separations of coordinate variables [7], parameter tuning [2–5,14–16], and inverse construction [8,9]. BICs are decoupled from extended waves, featuring ultrahigh quality factor (Q factor). This grants extreme sensitivity for potential high-performance sensing applications, but also makes them fragile to fabrication imperfections.

The rapid advancements in topological physics, spanning from condensed matter physics [20,21] to photonics [22,23] and acoustics [24–28], hold promise for addressing this challenge. Thanks to nontrivial band topology, topological edge states (TESs) can exhibit immunity to certain types of defects [23–25]. The fusion of topology and BICs not only broadens bulk-boundary correspondences, but also enhances BICs' robustness with inherent protection [29–43]. Existing research on TBICs follows two canonical approaches: one leverages mirror stacking to form a bilayer system (physically adding extra subspaces), inheriting energy spectrum and band topology from the original monolayer [29–36]. The other approach creates higher-order topological systems, where zeroth-order corner states coexist with second-order bulk states [37–43]. In both cases, TBICs rely on higher-dimensional complex systems. Moreover, even though there has been much interest in investigating the fundamental physics of TBICs [29–43], little attention has been paid to their application potentials.

In this Letter, we unveil the universality of TBICs by leveraging mode orthogonality encoded by symmetry [Fig. 1(a)]. We illustrate this conceptually and demonstrate it experimentally using a simple monolayered topological waveguide, whose symmetry naturally supports two independent subspaces. The eTBIC built in this way encodes extreme anisotropy in its transmission spectrum, exhibiting high sensitivity to the selected perturbations while remaining robust against others [Fig. 1(b)]. Our findings will enrich the research implications of both topological physics and BICs.

## Core Concept

The core concept of this work is outlined in Fig. 1(a). TBICs arise when two states—the topological edge state and the bulk state—coexist in momentum space without hybridization. The crux lies in the complete decoupling of corresponding states at the same energy level in physical space—a result of symmetry-induced mode orthogonality. Imagine a topological system accommodating vertical mirror-symmetric ($\Sigma$) and antisymmetric ($\Pi$) modes, acoustic analogs of *p* orbitals [44]. Mode orthogonality enables independent regulation of band structure positions, allowing coexistence of edge states without hybridization, leading to TBIC [Fig. 1(a)]. An intriguing scenario unfolds when the edge state associated with the $\Sigma$ mode becomes embedded within the bulk of the $\Pi$ mode. As we delve into the subsequent analysis, this specific type of TBIC exhibits a counterintuitive behavior: it appears as a pronounced peak in the transmission spectrum (in contrast to usual TBICs that remain "muted" within the transmission bulk). Termed the exotic TBIC (eTBIC), this state embodies seemingly contradictory features inherent to topological systems and BIC physics—namely, strong robustness and high sensitivity. In this way, the eTBIC encoding extreme anisotropic sensitivity in its transmission spectrum, responding intensely to certain perturbations [as depicted by transmission peak splitting in the right panel of Fig. 1(b)] while maintaining resilience to other disorders.

## Acoustic Waveguide System

To illustrate the proposed concept, we consider a simple framework—an acoustic waveguide with periodic cavities, as shown in Fig. 2(a) (see details in Supplemental Material, Sec. A [45]). The waveguide possesses two essential symmetries: discrete translational symmetry and lateral mirror symmetry. The discrete translational symmetry allows $\Sigma$ modes within the waveguide, exhibiting a folded dispersion. The lateral mirror symmetry supports $\Pi$ modes primarily localized in the cavities. There is a large difference between modes $\Pi$ and $\Sigma$ in waveguide systems: $\Sigma$ modes are symmetrically the same as the fundamental waveguide modes—plane waves. $\Pi$ modes are the higher-order waveguide modes that can exist in a single-cavity waveguide but cannot propagate. However, in the waveguide with periodic cavities, $\Pi$ modes originally concentrated in cavities can be interconnected through near-field coupling and thus extend throughout the whole lattice, forming bulk bands. The difference between modes $\Pi$ and $\Sigma$ set the stage for the distinct responses of TBICs and eTBIC in waveguide systems.

### Band Structure and Topology

To introduce topology, we take a band folding approach by adjusting the distance between adjacent cavities, as marked by dashed boxes in Fig. 2(a). The band structures of our system can be approximately described by an acoustic analog of the Su-Schrieffer-Heeger model [46]. In momentum space, the effective Hamiltonian takes the form

$$H(k) = \begin{pmatrix}
\omega_\Sigma & \kappa_\Sigma & 0 & 0 \\
\kappa_\Sigma^* & \omega_\Sigma & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & \omega_\Pi & \kappa_\Pi \\
0 & 0 & \kappa_\Pi^* & \omega_\Pi
\end{pmatrix},$$
(1)

where the hopping term $\kappa_i = v_i + u_i e^{-jk}$ involves intracell ($v_i$) and intercell ($u_i$) hoppings. The frequencies $\omega_i$ correspond to $\Sigma$ and $\Pi$ modes within a unit cell.

Symmetry-protected orthogonality between $\Sigma$ and $\Pi$ modes naturally partitions the effective Hamiltonian into two independent blocks [as shown in Eq. (1)], representing fully decoupled subspaces. By continuously tuning the displacement ($\varepsilon$) of the two cavities within a unit cell, a topological trivial or nontrivial band gap can be opened around the degenerate point at $\varepsilon = 0$, associated with band inversion. This applies to both $\Sigma$ and $\Pi$ modes, creating two uncoupled topological subspaces. In Fig. 2(a), these bands are colored according to their symmetries: blue for $\Sigma$ modes and red for $\Pi$ modes. By doing so, the topological edge state of $\Sigma$ can be engineered to reside within the bulk of $\Pi$ with zero leakage (or vice versa), leading to a TBIC, as conceptually sketched in Fig. 1(a). Figures 2(b) and 2(c) show clearly TESs and bulk states for both $\Pi$ and $\Sigma$ modes.

## Topological Phase Engineering

The proposed TBIC, achieved by leveraging symmetry, offers flexibility in engineering topological band structures within subspaces without hybridization. We demonstrate this by adjusting cavity height $C$ (see the impact of other parameters in Supplemental Material, Sec. A [45]). In Fig. 3(a), the phase diagram in the parameter space of frequency and $C$ reveals the evolution of topological states. In Fig. 3(a), we identify four regions:

- **TES (gray)**: no overlap occurs between edge states and the bulks of $\Pi$ and $\Sigma$ modes. Gray regions represent the common scenario where $\Pi$ and $\Sigma$ modes independently support their own edge states without interaction.

- **TBIC (orange)**: edge states of $\Pi$ reside within the bulk of $\Sigma$. These regions correspond to common TBICs submerged within the transmission bulk spectrum, making them undetectable.

- **TF (yellow)**: a specific state where edge states of $\Pi$ and $\Sigma$ degenerate, which triggers topological Fano (TF) resonance upon perturbation [47].

- **eTBIC (green)**: edge states of $\Sigma$ are embedded in the bulk of $\Pi$, which are intriguing topological states as will be shown in the following.

Figure 3(b) displays the corresponding energy spectrum for these different topological states.

## Transmission Spectrum and Unique Behavior

The unique behavior of eTBIC in this waveguide system is encoded in its transmission spectrum. Because of symmetry mismatch with incident waves (plane waves having vertical mirror symmetry), the bulk of $\Pi$ modes remains unexcited and thus cannot be observed in the transmission spectrum. However, leakage of a TES in a finite-length lattice [48–50] and symmetry matching allow the eTBIC to be clearly visible—a transmission peak manifests itself. Therefore, this configuration unambiguously pinpoints the occurrence of eTBIC, where the bulk remains "muted," and only the embedded edge state is observable, with a theoretical-predicted efficiency reaching 100%, as shown by the transmission spectrum at $\delta = 0$ mm in Fig. 4(b). To confirm the uniqueness of the eTBIC, we conduct a controlled study for TBIC, where the waveguide supporting a TBIC is under antisymmetric excitations [Fig. 4(c)]. For the TBIC case, neither the bulk nor the edge can be observed in the transmission spectrum.

## Anisotropic Sensitivity

We explore the anisotropic sensitivity of eTBIC under perturbation through simulations, ignoring the intrinsic losses first for simplicity. We introduce perturbations to the cavities in two ways: axial hovering and lateral shifting [Fig. 4(a)]. Specifically, we shift the cavities from their original positions without altering their geometries. This perturbation randomly displaces all cavities in the lattice by $+\delta$, $-\delta$, or 0 (see details in Supplemental Material, Sec. C [45]). Note that the axial perturbation is orthonormally decoupled from the (bulk) band of $\Pi$, while the edge state of $\Sigma$ is topologically protected. Consequently, the eTBIC exhibits robustness against axial perturbations, as evidenced by a steady transmission peak regardless of the degree of perturbation [Fig. 4(b)]. However, when perturbations are induced laterally, this symmetrically protected BIC becomes inherently fragile and is abruptly destroyed. Accordingly, the edge state of $\Sigma$ hybridizes with the bulk state of $\Pi$ at the same energy level, resulting in the emergence of two new hybrid modes.

As a controlled study, the behavior of TBIC under perturbation is found to be very different. Since the TBIC is not observable in the transmission, perturbing it will yield negligible results, regardless of whether the perturbations are axial or lateral [Fig. 4(c)]. The study of Q factors for TBIC and eTBIC helps to understand their distinct transmission responses. On the one hand, for bulks, symmetric states ($\Sigma$) have lower Q factors than antisymmetric states ($\Pi$), as illustrated by the slopes ($d\omega/dk$) of bands of both states shown in Fig. 2(a). On the other hand, in the waveguide system, (e)TBIC may couple to external excitations due to the finite size of the lattice. Because of leakage, (e)TBIC has a finite Q factor. For a certain symmetry, the edge state naturally has a much higher Q factor than the bulk state. Therefore, eTBIC and TBIC are clearly different in the Q factors between their respective edge states and bulks embedded (see details about the Q factors and the responses to perturbations in Supplemental Material, Secs. A and B [45]). Note that TBIC has a Q factor of up to $10^{5.7}$, meaning that the relative bandwidth of the transmission spectrum supported by TBIC is theoretically only $10^{-5.7}$. Therefore, the transmission peak of TBIC is unobservable in practice once intrinsic losses are considered.

## Experimental Verification

We then carry out experiments to investigate the behavior of eTBIC. Three sets of experimental samples, with lateral, axial, and global (i.e., lateral and axial) perturbations, are fabricated. Each set consists of nine samples, whose perturbation levels are gradually increased with a step of 0.1 mm (1/1000 of the operating wavelength). Our experimental setup is shown in Fig. 5(a). We employ the transfer matrix method [51] to calculate the transmission spectrum. As a control group, a specimen without perturbations is also tested for comparison. The simulated transmission spectrum of the specimen [Fig. 5(c), blue line] and the measured data (blue dots) unambiguously confirm the occurrence of the eTBIC, as expected. In contrast, the transmission spectrum of a global perturbed specimen with $\delta = 1$ mm clearly exhibits splitting peaks [Fig. 5(c), red line and red dots]. Note that, as shown by experimental results, when we consider the intrinsic loss to the system, two hybridized edge states exhibit different transmission peak heights, which suggests that loss may be an additional degree of freedom for the proposed eTBIC-based sensing (see Supplemental Material, Fig. S20 [45]).

As shown in Fig. 5(d), the sensitivity, quantified by the splitting of peaks in three sets of experimental samples, is summarized by the dots with different colors. We compare this sensitivity with those subjected to only axial (blue) or lateral (red) perturbations, extracted from simulated results (solid lines). For the first two sets of samples with only axial or lateral perturbations, the experimental data are in good agreement with the simulated structure. The experimental results of the third set of samples shows that, although these specimens experience global perturbations in both axial and lateral directions, only the lateral components can be sensitively detected (in good agreement with the lateral results). This remarkable result provides a solid evidence for the exotic behavior of the eTBIC combining both sensitivity and robustness (see more experimental details in Supplemental Material, Sec. C [45]).

## Conclusion

To conclude, our work demonstrates the realization of TBICs in an acoustic monolayer topological waveguide by capitalizing on the universality of mode orthogonality arising from symmetry. Our design's remarkable tunability allows simultaneous engineering of topological band structures within each subspace without hybridization. This versatility results in rich topological features, including TESs, TBICs, TF, and eTBICs. We further discovered that the eTBIC reconciles seemingly opposing concepts—strong immunity and high sensitivity—deeply rooted in the nature of topological states and BICs, respectively. This imparts extremely anisotropic sensitivity, allowing the eTBIC to vigilantly response to selected perturbations while maintaining resilience against other "environmental noise." Our findings may enrich the research implications of both topological systems and BIC physics, opening up the possibility of TBIC-based sensing.
